The Opportunity

AI will displace a lot of work.
That's an opening, if we build for it.

The Institute started from a simple conviction: the disruption AI brings to work and society doesn't have to be a catastrophe to survive. It could be the greatest opportunity in our species' history — if enough people help build toward it deliberately instead of leaving the outcome to chance, or to whoever moves first.

We don't think any single person, including the founder of this site, should be the one deciding what that future looks like. What follows is a set of brainstormed starting ideas — a floor under every person with no ceiling on anyone, work that's actually meaningful, technology that stays a tool instead of becoming a master. They're offered as a conversation starter, not a conclusion.

The real model only gets built with whole communities in the room, testing these ideas against real life and replacing the ones that don't hold up. That's what this Institute exists to convene.

Starting Ideas, Not Final Answers

Five ideas we're brainstorming in public

Each of these began as one person's draft. None of them are settled. They're here to be picked apart, stress-tested, and rebuilt by people who'd actually have to live inside them.

A Floor, Not a Ceiling

One proposal: guarantee food, shelter, healthcare, and meaningful work for everyone, while touching nothing anyone has already built. Worth arguing about: where exactly that floor sits.

Technology as a Tool, Not a Master

A starting line: AI helps catch corruption and audit power, and never watches ordinary people. We think this line matters more than almost anything else here — tell us if we're wrong.

Community Architecture

A sketch of shared meals, shared land, and shared governance at human scale — the part of this that probably can't be designed from one desk, and needs to be built by the communities living it.

Business Beyond Profit

A rough idea for rewarding organizations that genuinely improve people's lives, not just the ones that extract value fastest. The hard part — how you measure that fairly — is wide open.

Dignity and Public Safety, Together

A position to argue with: accountability for harm and dignity for the person aren't actually opposites. Where exactly that balance sits is a community decision, not ours to make alone.

The Full Draft

Everything brainstormed so far

The five ideas above, written out in full — plus the rest of what we've sketched so far, including a starting idea for how people might find meaningful work in a more automated economy. Every chapter includes the reasoning behind it and the honest objections we could think of ourselves. It covers the transition we're in, the work-optional vision, community architecture, responsible technology, business beyond profit, justice and liberty, and how this could actually get built, starting small.

Get Involved

This only works if whole communities help build it

One person's brainstorm, however well-intentioned, has blind spots no amount of solo thinking will find. These ideas need to be stress-tested by teachers, farmers, nurses, business owners, parents, and skeptics — the people who'd actually have to live inside whatever gets built. That's not a focus group. That's co-authorship.

01

Pick One Idea

You don't need an opinion on all six. Start with whichever one touches something you actually know about — your work, your town, your kids' future.

02

Tell Us What Breaks

Agreement is nice, but specific disagreement is what actually builds something real. Tell us where it falls apart in practice, not just in theory.

03

Help Rebuild It

The best fixes get folded into the next draft, with credit and a public changelog — so this stays a shared project, not a solo one wearing a community's name.

Founder & Executive Director

Nick Duda

The Institute is fortunate to have the work and ideas of our Founder and Executive Director, Nick Duda, guiding its early growth. Nick is the author of Sawyer's Reach: The Battle for Tomorrow and The Modern Teacher's Handbook, and has spent more than 22 years designing and leading learning environments in high-poverty, high-ESL districts near Chicago — work that means building systems for real human needs under real constraints, every day, for two decades. He holds a Master of Science in Education and seven Illinois teaching certifications.

He started the Institute because he didn't think this conversation should wait for someone else to start it — or be decided by any one person, himself included. The ideas on this page are an opening draft, offered so a real, wide conversation has somewhere to begin.

That same throughline — technology, power, and what we owe each other — runs through both of his books, his teaching, and now this Institute.
